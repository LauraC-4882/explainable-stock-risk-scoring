# How much does "passed Kupiec" actually tell us?

Generated by `scripts/kupiec_power.py` (exact enumeration over the binomial
distribution — deterministic, no simulation). Every figure below is reproduced
by running that script; none is typed in by hand.

Two parts. The first answers a power question. The second explains why a second
power question should not be asked at all.

## Part 1 — Power against a coverage miss

The alternative Kupiec is built for: the true breach rate is not 5%. The
decision rule replicates the suite's exactly — `LR_uc` compared against
`chi2(1)`, reject when `p < 0.05` (`validation/tail_tests.py:103`).

Sample sizes are the ones the suite actually grades, after the 100-day estimator
warm-up and the one-day forecast shift.

### The 6% column is the finding

**Every snapshot has power below 0.20 against a true rate of 6%.** A VaR line
that is wrong by a fifth of its target — 6% of days breaching where 5% was
claimed — is caught less than one time in five at this sample size.

Stated plainly: at n ~ 430, "passed Kupiec" is materially weaker evidence than
the phrase suggests. It rules out a *gross* coverage miss. It does not rule out
the kind of miscalibration a reader would still care about. This is not a
criticism of the test — it is what a binomial count test can do with 430
observations — but it does mean a passing result should be reported as "no gross
miss detected", never as "coverage is correct".

Even at 7%, a 40% relative error in coverage, the test misses more often than it
fires (power 0.45–0.49). Reasonable power arrives only around 8% (0.74–0.79).

### Why the pooled row is an upper bound and nothing more

Pooling reaches 0.61 at 6% and is essentially certain by 7%. That number is
**not** attainable in practice, and it is labelled as a bound rather than a
result.

Kupiec's binomial likelihood assumes independent observations. The panel is four
A-shares, a CSI 300 ETF, and one US name — the A-shares and the ETF that tracks
their index share market days and move together, so breaches co-occur rather
than arriving independently. Treating 2,618 correlated observations as 2,618
independent trials overstates the effective sample size and therefore the power.

The size of the overstatement is not estimated here: doing so honestly requires
modelling the cross-sectional dependence, which is a larger piece of work and
outside this analysis. What can be said without that work is the direction —
the true pooled power lies somewhere between the single-snapshot figures and the
row above, and closer to the former than the pooled n suggests.

### Decision rule

The suite uses the chi-square asymptotic rule. An exact two-sided binomial rule
was computed alongside it as a check on whether the reported power depends on
that choice.

**Largest disagreement across every snapshot and alternative: 0.0020.** The two
rules agree to within a fifth of a percentage point everywhere, so nothing in
Part 1 rests on the asymptotic approximation.

## Part 2 — What this test cannot see

This section is a result, not a limitation notice.

### Structural zero power against a fatter tail at unchanged coverage

`LR_uc` is a function of the breach *count* `x` and the sample size `n`:

```
LR_uc = -2[(n-x)log(1-α) + x·log(α) - (n-x)log(1-x/n) - x·log(x/n)]
```

`x` is a sum of indicators — no return magnitude enters. So consider any
alternative that changes the shape of the left tail while leaving the breach
probability at 5%: losses beyond the VaR line become larger, but they occur just
as often. Under that alternative the distribution of `x` is unchanged, therefore
the distribution of `LR_uc` is unchanged pointwise, therefore the rejection
probability equals the significance level — **0.05, for every n**.

That is not low power. It is zero power, in the sense that the rejection
probability under the alternative equals the rejection probability under the
null. Increasing the sample size to any size whatsoever does not change it, and
a power curve for this alternative would be a flat line at 0.05.

### The same argument covers clustering

`x` is invariant to the *order* of the breach indicators. Any alternative that
reorders breaches — clustering them into volatile stretches instead of spreading
them out — leaves `x` unchanged and so leaves `LR_uc` unchanged. Same
conclusion, same reason.

### AAPL is the worked example, already in the results

| test | AAPL result |
|---|---|
| Kupiec POF | p = 0.4016, **pass** — 28 breaches, 5.86%, right about the target |
| Christoffersen independence | p = 0.0038, **REJECT** — breaches follow breaches 4.4x more often than they follow calm days |

The count is right and the structure is wrong, in the same series, at the same
time. AAPL passing Kupiec is not evidence that AAPL's tail is well behaved; it
is evidence that Kupiec was asked a different question.

### Which is why the suite runs four tests

| question | test | what it reads |
|---|---|---|
| Do breaches happen at the claimed rate? | Kupiec POF | the count |
| Do they arrive independently? | Christoffersen independence | the order |
| Both at once? | Christoffersen conditional coverage | count and order jointly |
| When a breach happens, is the loss the size ES predicted? | Acerbi-Székely Z2 | the magnitudes |

Each test is blind to what the others measure, by construction rather than by
oversight. Running all four is not redundancy — dropping any one leaves a class
of miscalibration that nothing else in the suite would notice.

## What this analysis does not establish

- No claim about whether the VaR line is well calibrated. This measures the
  *test's* ability to detect miscalibration, not the presence or absence of it.
- No effective sample size for the pooled row (see above).
- Power is computed under the model's own assumption of independent
  observations *within* a series. Autocorrelated breaches within one ticker
  would reduce the single-snapshot figures too, in the same direction as the
  pooled caveat.
