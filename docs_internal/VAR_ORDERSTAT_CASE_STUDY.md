# The 21-day VaR feature is an order statistic: a case study

Generated by `scripts/var_orderstat_case_study.py` at base `a67eb22`, on the
six-ticker sample declared in `snapshots/validation_manifest.txt`. Every table
below is that script's output, pasted verbatim; the regions are marked so the
document can be diffed against a fresh run byte for byte. No figure is typed
in by hand.

## Scope

The session ledger's packaging rule scopes only the point-in-time audit's
headline finding on the ML leg; this case study is outside that scope, carries
no point-in-time content, and makes no walk-forward claim.

Three questions, in order:

1. Which plotting position does each VaR series in this codebase use, and what
   exceedance rate does that position imply? Both series are stated, because
   `93b5871` corrected one and deliberately left the other.
2. What does the tail suite say about the reported VaR at this base, once the
   six tests per family are read with multiplicity in mind?
3. The scorer never displays the 21-day series; it ranks it against the stock's
   own history. Does that ranking wash the estimator's bias out?

The third question is tested here, not settled. No fix is proposed.

## 1. Two estimators, two plotting positions

A sample quantile at level p from n observations is a position h in the sorted
sample, and the position decides the exceedance rate. The next draw falls below
the k-th order statistic of n with probability k/(n+1) under exchangeability,
whatever the distribution.

The **reported** VaR, `var_95_100d`, is what the scorecard shows and the tail
suite grades. Since `93b5871` it is a 100-day rolling quantile at the Weibull
position h = p(n+1) - 1 = 4.05, which is the position for which the nominal
and actual exceedance rates agree at any window length. That commit also fixed
the window: 21 observations cannot estimate a 5% quantile at all.

The **scoring feature**, `var_95_21d`, is not the reported VaR and was left
exactly as it was. It is `rolling(21).quantile(0.05)` with pandas' default
position h = p(n-1) = 0.05 x 20 = 1.0. That is an integer, so no interpolation
happens: the value is the second smallest of the last 21 returns, an order
statistic with k = 2, exceeded with probability 2/22 = 9.09% at any tail
thickness. The script asserts this equality on every row of every snapshot
before it computes anything else. The feature was retained because it feeds
the trained artefact, the Tail Risk factor and the stress multipliers, where
responsiveness and ordering matter and the score is a percentile against the
stock's own history. What was wrong was calling it a 95% VaR; the reported
95% VaR is the 100-day Weibull series above.

| series | window | position | k-th of n | implied exceedance | role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| reported VaR (`var_95_100d`) | 100 | Weibull, h = 4.05 | between 5th and 6th of 100 | 5.00% | shown on the card; graded by the tail suite |
| 21-day scoring feature | 21 | pandas default, h = 1.0 | exactly the 2nd of 21 | 9.09% | scoring input only; ranked against own history; not the reported VaR |

Both positions are stated because they lead to different questions. The
reported series is graded directly (section 2). The feature is never graded
against a 5% target, because 9.09% is what its position returns by
construction; the only question worth asking about it is whether the ranking
step downstream neutralises the position (section 3).

Reading the Kupiec column with that in mind: `301189_SZ` at p = 0.0280 fails
the nominal 0.05 and survives the Bonferroni threshold, so on its own it is one
rejection among six where a quarter of the time one is expected under six true
nulls. The pooled run does not reject (p = 0.1833).

The independence column is different. `AAPL` at p = 0.0038 survives both
thresholds: breaches follow breaches about 4.4 times as often as they follow
calm days (22 calm-to-breach transitions against 6 breach-to-breach, from
`n01` and `n11` in the JSON). This is the finding that was always in the data
and was previously drowned out by the estimator's position; it is a property
of the reported series, and no re-specification of an unconditional quantile
removes it.

The Z2 column is one-sided and bootstrapped, and its severity ratios sit
between 1.00 and 1.16: on breach days the realised loss averaged up to 16%
worse than the ES line predicted. Four of the six reach the nominal level;
`301189_SZ` and `AAPL` also clear Bonferroni. What Kupiec can and cannot see at
these sample sizes is the subject of `docs_internal/KUPIEC_POWER_ANALYSIS.md`
and is not repeated here.

## 3. Wash-out under own-history percentile ranking

The argument for leaving the 21-day scoring feature alone is that the score is
a percentile against the stock's own history, which absorbs a time-constant
level bias. An order-statistic bias is not a constant shift, though. The second
smallest of 21 differs from the Weibull position for the same window by
0.9 x (second smallest minus smallest), which scales with how spread out the
window's left tail is. A ranking step can only absorb it if that spread is
itself stable through the history being ranked over.

### Method

Stated in full because there is no committed prior definition.

- **Feature.** `var_95_21d`, the scoring feature exactly as `RiskMetrics`
  computes it: the second order statistic of the trailing 21 log returns.
- **Comparator.** The same 21 returns at the Weibull position,
  `np.quantile(window, 0.05, method="weibull")`, h = 0.1: the window minimum
  plus a tenth of the gap to the second smallest.
- **Ranking.** The scorer's rule, replayed on an expanding window:
  `scipy.stats.percentileofscore` with `kind="mean"`, direction -1 (more
  negative is riskier), no rank until 20 observations exist, and the current
  value included in its own history. This is what `risk_categories` would have
  shown on each day given the history it had.
- **Ratio.** Feature divided by comparator, both negative, so it lies in (0, 1]
  and equals 1 only when the two smallest returns coincide.
- **Rank gap.** Absolute difference between the two ranked series, in rank
  units (0.10 is ten percentile points).
- **Terciles.** Of the trailing 21-day standard deviation of log returns, the
  same window as the feature. Cutoffs are **per ticker**: each ticker's days are
  split into its own thirds. The pooled-cutoff variant, one split across all
  2,624 ticker-days, is printed alongside because the two disagree in the high
  tercile (see the open discrepancy below).

Under per-ticker cutoffs the pattern is monotone in volatility: the ratio falls
from 0.753 to 0.690, the mean rank gap rises from 0.111 to 0.205, and the
Spearman correlation between the two ranked series falls from 0.766 to 0.442.
In calm stretches the feature and the comparator rank a day within about eleven
percentile points of each other on average; in volatile stretches the gap is
about twenty points, and the two rankings agree only loosely. Per ticker, the
raw series correlate at 0.665 to 0.783 and the ranked series at 0.689 to 0.802.
The ranking step narrows the disagreement slightly; it does not remove it.

### Two caveats, and why the conclusion stops short

**The comparator is itself noisy.** At h = 0.1 the Weibull position on 21
observations is essentially the window minimum, which is the single least
stable order statistic there is. The last two columns of the per-ticker table
make the point: against the 100-day reported VaR, the comparator's Spearman
runs 0.186 to 0.636 and the scoring feature's 0.089 to 0.518, and neither is
consistently the better tracker. The rank gap therefore measures two noisy
21-day estimators disagreeing with each other, not the feature's distance from
a well-estimated quantile. A gap that grows with volatility is what two noisy
estimators would show even if neither were biased.

**Open discrepancy in the high tercile.** An earlier, uncommitted run of this
comparison reported ratios of 0.751 / 0.702 / 0.686 and rank gaps of 0.116 /
0.180 / 0.187. Every cell here is within snapshot-refresh distance of those
except the high-tercile rank gap, 0.205 against 0.187. Its method was never
recorded, so the difference cannot be attributed to drift or to a definition
difference, and it is listed as open rather than explained. The pooled-cutoff
table is printed for that reason: under one split across all tickers the high
tercile is 0.175 and is not the worst tercile at all, so the high-tercile
figure is sensitive to a choice this document had to make. The direction of
the per-ticker pattern is not sensitive to it; its size is.

**Conclusion: tested, not established.** The hypothesis that own-history
percentile ranking washes out the order-statistic position was tested on the
manifest sample and the evidence points against it, more so in volatile
regimes. That evidence rests on a comparator whose own noise is of the same
order as the effect, and on a tercile definition that moves the headline cell.
Nothing here proposes changing the feature, retraining anything, or altering
what the card shows; the point of the document is that the claim now has a
measurement attached, and a measurement is what any later decision should be
made against.

```bash
.venv/bin/python scripts/var_orderstat_case_study.py --json /tmp/case_study.json
```

The script prints regions A and B above, in that order, separated by one blank
line; the document's two generated regions concatenated the same way must equal
that output byte for byte. It reads the manifest sample only, seeds nothing but
the Z2 bootstrap's fixed seed inside `validation/tail_tests.py`, and never
touches the network.
